## Abstract

This paper examines how feature correlation affects the speed of Lasso solvers and the evaluation of support recovery. To test whether the support obtained at convergence is unique, we propose a column permutation experiment that leaves the objective unchanged. Using a normalised duality gap as a common stopping criterion, we compare ISTA, FISTA, coordinate descent, sklearn, and skglm. On synthetic designs, correlation slows the basic solvers by more than an order of magnitude, while the working set solver skglm slows down at most threefold. Once the duality gap falls to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-6} &#92;)</span>, all methods return the same support. On UJIIndoorLoc features solved to a gap of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-10} &#92;)</span>, the experiment shows that the support still changes with the input column order and that F1 varies by up to 9.63 percentage points. These features contain nearly identical columns, so the problem has multiple solutions that differ in how coefficients are split within a group, and the outputs agree once coefficients are summed by group. We propose an evaluation procedure that finds these groups before fitting, certifies a unique solution from one approximate solve through a duality gap bound on the equicorrelation set, and otherwise scores supports by group. The certificate holds on news popularity and gene expression data, where every column order gives the same support.

## 1 Introduction
{: #sec-intro}

The Lasso [[1]](#ref-1) adds an <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;ell_1 &#92;)</span> penalty to the least-squares loss and performs coefficient estimation and variable selection together. It is widely used for high-dimensional sparse regression. In applications such as gene expression analysis and WiFi fingerprinting, the selected variables are often interpreted as disease genes or key access points, and methods are compared by the precision, recall, and F1 of support recovery.

Features in such data are often highly correlated. Correlation increases the Lipschitz constant of the smooth part of the loss and slows proximal gradient methods. Correlated features carry similar information, and the Lasso tends to pick one of them [[2]](#ref-2). When the features are not in general position, several optimal solutions can produce one set of fitted values through different supports [[3]](#ref-3). Solvers usually judge convergence by the objective value or the duality gap [[4]](#ref-4), [[5]](#ref-5), and recovery studies then compare the converged output, taken as the estimator, with the true support [[6]](#ref-6), [[7]](#ref-7). This pipeline requires the support to be determined once the objective has converged. We test this requirement on correlated designs.

We first compare the convergence speed of ISTA, FISTA, coordinate descent (CD), and two modern solvers under different levels of correlation, and track how the support stabilises as the gap decreases. All implementations stop on the same duality gap, and the tests cover synthetic designs and real feature matrices with simulated responses. We then use a column permutation experiment to test whether the support is uniquely determined when the implementation, the accuracy, and the objective are all held fixed. On UJIIndoorLoc [[8]](#ref-8) features, the same solver at a gap of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-10} &#92;)</span> gives F1 values that differ by up to 9.63 percentage points across column orders, while the fitted values of the outputs differ only at the level of floating-point error.

The two experiments probe two properties of the same convex problem. Speed depends on conditioning. ISTA and FISTA are proximal gradient methods, FISTA adds Nesterov acceleration, and CD updates one coordinate at a time. Once the support is identified, ISTA converges linearly at a rate set by the restricted strong convexity on that support, and correlation lowers this curvature. Uniqueness of the support depends on strict convexity. The Lasso objective is not strictly convex when columns are linearly dependent, so the solution set can contain several points. The Fenchel dual gives a gap that certifies the objective value, while the choice among these solutions is left to the solver.

Our contributions are as follows.

- We compare proximal gradient, coordinate descent, and working set solvers on the same problems using the same duality gap stopping rule. The slowdown caused by correlation is set against the growth of the Lipschitz constant, and the iteration counts follow the linear rate set by the curvature on the selected support. We also report the accuracy at which the support becomes stable and the extra time needed to reach it.
- We design an experiment that reorders the input columns and maps the coefficients back, leaving the optimisation problem untouched. Reordering changes the selected variables and their F1 on the UJI design, and leaves the selection intact on news and gene expression data.
- We trace the order sensitivity to nearly proportional feature groups and propose an evaluation procedure built on this mechanism. A scan before fitting finds the groups, and a duality gap bound gives a superset of the equicorrelation set from one approximate solution, whose full column rank certifies a unique solution. The certificate holds for all 144 news and gene outputs, and on UJI, merging the detected groups makes the two reference solutions agree on 12 of 12 targets.

## 2 Related Work
{: #sec-related}

**Proximal gradient and coordinate descent.** ISTA grew out of the thresholding iteration of Daubechies et al. [[9]](#ref-9) for linear inverse problems and is the proximal gradient method applied to the Lasso. FISTA [[4]](#ref-4) uses Nesterov extrapolation to improve the bound on the objective residual from <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(1/k) &#92;)</span> to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(1/k^2) &#92;)</span>, at the cost of a possibly nonmonotone objective. Coordinate descent exploits the closed-form soft-thresholding update. Tseng [[10]](#ref-10) gave convergence conditions for separable nonsmooth problems, and glmnet [[11]](#ref-11) made it the standard Lasso solver through incremental residual updates and warm starts along the path. It has since been extended to all generalized linear models [[12]](#ref-12) and, in adelie [[13]](#ref-13), to block coordinate path solvers for the group Lasso and elastic net. On acceleration, Bertrand and Massias [[14]](#ref-14) applied Anderson extrapolation to coordinate descent, and skglm [[5]](#ref-5) combines working sets with this accelerated CD in a modular framework. Larsson et al. [[15]](#ref-15) designed a hybrid of proximal gradient and coordinate descent for the sorted-<span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;ell_1 &#92;)</span> penalty SLOPE.

**Duality gap, screening, and stopping criteria.** Fercoq et al. [[16]](#ref-16) and Ndiaye et al. [[17]](#ref-17) built safe screening rules from the primal–dual gap that discard coordinates guaranteed to be zero during optimization. Walwil and Fercoq [[18]](#ref-18) analyzed the error bounds and computational cost of using the duality gap and its smoothed variant as a stopping criterion. We adopt the duality gap as the common stopping criterion across implementations and ask whether a sufficiently small gap also determines the support.

**Support recovery under correlated designs.** Classical theory gives the irrepresentable condition for Lasso model selection consistency [[19]](#ref-19) and sharp thresholds in sample size, sparsity, and noise [[6]](#ref-6). The elastic net [[2]](#ref-2) introduced a grouping effect in response to the Lasso picking one variable from a highly correlated group. Recent work refines the role of correlation geometry: Kumar et al. [[7]](#ref-7) numerically characterise support recovery under correlated designs, and Stallrich et al. [[20]](#ref-20) study sign recovery from the viewpoint of experimental design. Minsker and Shen [[21]](#ref-21) analyze the average case under contamination and correlated designs, and Buhai [[22]](#ref-22) studies the geometry between active and inactive variables in partially rotated designs. These studies take the solver output as the estimator when analyzing statistical recovery.

**Solution multiplicity, trustworthy computation, and column order.** Tibshirani [[3]](#ref-3) proved that all Lasso solutions share the same fitted values and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;ell_1 &#92;)</span> norm, and that the support can vary among solutions when the features are not in general position. Bu et al. [[23]](#ref-23) deliberately use multiple nearly optimal solutions to study selection among highly correlated variables. Bastounis et al. [[24]](#ref-24), [[25]](#ref-25) characterize, through condition numbers, how hard it is to compute the Lasso support reliably in finite precision, and relate random data, conditioning, and statistical recovery. On ordering, Li and Osher [[26]](#ref-26) compared sequential and greedy sweeps for <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;ell_1 &#92;)</span> problems and their effect on the sparse path. Kim et al. [[27]](#ref-27) proved a rate advantage of random permutations in each epoch over uniform sampling in coordinate descent. The knockpy package [[28]](#ref-28) randomly permutes the input columns before fitting the Lasso and maps the result back afterwards to preserve knockoff symmetry. Nouraie et al. [[29]](#ref-29) show that column order changes the selected variables through Gram–Schmidt decorrelation. We hold the implementation, the gap accuracy, the objective, and the evaluation target fixed, and relate the effect of column order on support and F1 to nearly proportional structure in real designs.

## 3 Problem and Methods
{: #sec-problem-method}

### 3.1 Problem Formulation
{: #sec-formulation}

Given <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X&#92;in&#92;mathbb{R}^{n&#92;times p} &#92;)</span> with standardized columns and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( y&#92;in&#92;mathbb{R}^n &#92;)</span>, the Lasso solves

<div id="eq-lasso" class="tape-equation" markdown="0">
\[
\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^p} P(w)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2n}\|y-Xw\|_2^2}_{f(w)}+\lambda\|w\|_1,\qquad\lambda&gt;0.
\tag{1}
\]
</div>

The gradient <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;nabla f(w)=X^&#92;top(Xw-y)/n &#92;)</span> is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( L &#92;)</span>-Lipschitz with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( L=&#92;&#124;X&#92;&#124;_2^2/n &#92;)</span>. Problem [(1)](#eq-lasso) is convex, and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;lambda&gt;0 &#92;)</span> makes the level sets bounded, so the solution set is nonempty. When <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( p&gt;n &#92;)</span> or the columns are linearly dependent, <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( f &#92;)</span> is not strongly convex, and the problem may have multiple solutions. The optimality condition is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 0&#92;in&#92;nabla f(w^&#92;star)+&#92;lambda&#92;partial&#92;&#124;w^&#92;star&#92;&#124;_1 &#92;)</span>, that is, <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#124;x_j^&#92;top r^&#92;star&#124;/n&#92;le&#92;lambda &#92;)</span> with equality whenever <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( w^&#92;star_j&#92;ne0 &#92;)</span>, where <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( r^&#92;star=y-Xw^&#92;star &#92;)</span>.

**Correlation and conditioning.** Consider an equicorrelated block of size <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( g &#92;)</span> and correlation <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho &#92;)</span>. Its population covariance has one eigenvalue equal to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 1+(g-1)&#92;rho &#92;)</span> and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( g-1 &#92;)</span> eigenvalues equal to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 1-&#92;rho &#92;)</span>. We set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( g=20 &#92;)</span>. As <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho &#92;)</span> goes from 0 to 0.9, the largest eigenvalue grows from 1 to 18.1, and the smallest eigenvalue in a block drops from 1 to 0.1. Under our data generator, the numerically computed <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( L=&#92;&#124;X&#92;&#124;_2^2/n &#92;)</span> grows from about 5.8 to about 30.6, so the proximal gradient step size <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 1/L &#92;)</span> shrinks by about a factor of 5. Coordinate descent scales each step by the curvature along a single coordinate, which is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;_2^2/n=1 &#92;)</span>.

**Duality gap.** By Fenchel duality, the dual of problem [(1)](#eq-lasso) is

<div id="eq-dual" class="tape-equation" markdown="0">
\[
\max_{u\in\mathbb{R}^n} D(u)=\frac{2y^\top u-\|u\|_2^2}{2n}\qquad\text{s.t. } \|X^\top u\|_\infty\le n\lambda .
\tag{2}
\]
</div>

Rescaling the current residual <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( r=y-Xw &#92;)</span> gives the feasible point <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( u=r&#92;cdot&#92;min&#92;{1,n&#92;lambda/&#92;&#124;X^&#92;top r&#92;&#124;_&#92;infty&#92;} &#92;)</span>, and weak duality gives <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G=P(w)-D(u)&#92;ge P(w)-P^&#92;star&#92;ge0 &#92;)</span>. We define <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}}=&#92;max(0,G)/&#92;max(1,&#124;P(w)&#124;,&#124;D(u)&#124;) &#92;)</span> as the common stopping and acceptance criterion for all implementations.

**Support and recovery metrics.** Semi-synthetic data follow <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( y=X&#92;beta^&#92;star+&#92;varepsilon &#92;)</span> with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;varepsilon&#92;sim&#92;mathcal{N}(0,&#92;sigma^2I) &#92;)</span> and true support <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( S^&#92;star=&#92;{j:&#92;beta^&#92;star_j&#92;ne0&#92;} &#92;)</span>. The output support is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( S_&#92;tau(w)=&#92;{j:&#124;w_j&#124;&gt;&#92;tau&#92;} &#92;)</span>, with main threshold <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;tau=10^{-6} &#92;)</span> and sensitivity threshold <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-4} &#92;)</span>. From <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;mathrm{TP},&#92;mathrm{FP},&#92;mathrm{FN} &#92;)</span> we compute <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( F_1=2&#92;mathrm{TP}/(2&#92;mathrm{TP}+&#92;mathrm{FP}+&#92;mathrm{FN}) &#92;)</span>. Exact recovery requires <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;mathrm{FP}=&#92;mathrm{FN}=0 &#92;)</span>. Disagreement between two outputs is measured by the support symmetric difference <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#124;S_a&#92;triangle S_b&#124; &#92;)</span>, the coefficient difference <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;&#124;w_a-w_b&#92;&#124;_&#92;infty &#92;)</span>, and the fit difference <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;&#124;X(w_a-w_b)&#92;&#124;_2/&#92;sqrt n &#92;)</span>.

### 3.2 Algorithms
{: #sec-method}

The soft-thresholding operator <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;mathcal{S}_a(z)=&#92;mathrm{sign}(z)&#92;max(&#124;z&#124;-a,0) &#92;)</span> is the proximal operator of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( a&#92;&#124;&#92;cdot&#92;&#124;_1 &#92;)</span> and is the basic operation of every method.

- **ISTA**: <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( w^{k+1}=&#92;mathcal{S}_{&#92;lambda/L}&#92;big(w^k-&#92;nabla f(w^k)/L&#92;big) &#92;)</span>, with cost <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(np) &#92;)</span> per step and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( P(w^k)-P^&#92;star&#92;le L&#92;&#124;w^0-w^&#92;star&#92;&#124;_2^2/(2k) &#92;)</span>.
- **FISTA** [[4]](#ref-4): the same proximal step taken at the extrapolated point <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( z^k=w^k+&#92;frac{t_{k-1}-1}{t_k}(w^k-w^{k-1}) &#92;)</span> with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( t_{k+1}=(1+&#92;sqrt{1+4t_k^2})/2 &#92;)</span>. Each step costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(np) &#92;)</span>, the objective residual is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(L/k^2) &#92;)</span>, and the objective can oscillate.
- **Cyclic coordinate descent (CD)**: maintain the residual <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( r &#92;)</span> and, for <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( j=1,&#92;dots,p &#92;)</span> in turn, set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( w_j^{+}=&#92;mathcal{S}_&#92;lambda(x_j^&#92;top r/n+a_jw_j)/a_j &#92;)</span> with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( a_j=&#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;_2^2/n &#92;)</span>, then update <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( r&#92;leftarrow r-x_j(w_j^+-w_j) &#92;)</span>. One epoch costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(np) &#92;)</span>, and the kernel is written in C99.
- **sklearn**: a mature cyclic CD implementation with an active set. **skglm** [[5]](#ref-5): selects a working set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( W &#92;)</span> by the size of optimality violations and runs CD with Anderson acceleration within it. Scoring the violations costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(np) &#92;)</span> per outer iteration, and each inner epoch costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(n&#124;W&#124;) &#92;)</span>.

The step size <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 1/L &#92;)</span> is estimated by power iteration. All experiments run in float64 on a single thread from a zero initialisation. The basic methods check <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}} &#92;)</span> every 5 iterations or 5 epochs.

**Local linear rate.** The bounds above hold for any convex <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( f &#92;)</span>. Proximal gradient iterations on the Lasso identify the support of the solution in finitely many steps [[30]](#ref-30). After that, ISTA acts as gradient descent on the coordinates in the selected set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( A &#92;)</span>, where <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( f &#92;)</span> has curvature at least <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;mu_A=&#92;lambda_{&#92;min}(X_A^&#92;top X_A/n) &#92;)</span>. The error then contracts by a factor of about <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 1-&#92;mu_A/L &#92;)</span> per step, so reducing <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}} &#92;)</span> by a factor of 100 takes about <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( (L/&#92;mu_A)&#92;ln 100 &#92;)</span> iterations. Correlation within <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( A &#92;)</span> lowers <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;mu_A &#92;)</span>, and correlation across the design raises <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( L &#92;)</span>. Both enter the same ratio.

### 3.3 Proposed Method for Order Invariant Evaluation
{: #sec-diagnostic}

The column order experiment detects order sensitivity with several solves per target. We propose a procedure that reaches the same decision from <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X &#92;)</span> and one approximate solution. It finds groups of nearly proportional columns, certifies uniqueness of the solution when it holds, and otherwise moves the evaluation to groups.

1. **Group scan before fitting.** Normalize the columns, compute <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#124;x_j^&#92;top x_k&#124;/(&#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;&#92;&#124;x_k&#92;&#124;) &#92;)</span> for all pairs in blocks of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( B &#92;)</span> columns, and admit <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( x_k &#92;)</span> to the group of anchor <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( x_j &#92;)</span> when <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;&#124;x_k-a&#92;,x_j&#92;&#124;_2/&#92;&#124;x_k&#92;&#124;_2&#92;le10^{-12} &#92;)</span> for the least-squares scale <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( a &#92;)</span>. This costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(np^2) &#92;)</span> time and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(Bp) &#92;)</span> memory and uses neither <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( y &#92;)</span> nor any fit.
2. **Uniqueness certificate after fitting.** Solve to a small gap and form the set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;bar E &#92;)</span> of Proposition [1](#prop-enclose). Since <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( E&#92;subseteq&#92;bar E &#92;)</span>, full column rank of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_{&#92;bar E} &#92;)</span> implies full column rank of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_E &#92;)</span>, and the Lasso solution is then unique [[3]](#ref-3). An SVD of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_{&#92;bar E} &#92;)</span> costs <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( O(n&#124;&#92;bar E&#124;^2) &#92;)</span>.
3. **Choice of evaluation target.** If the certificate holds, supports are scored per variable. Otherwise, when the deficient directions lie inside the groups from step 1, as on UJI, each group is replaced by its anchor column before fitting, or scored through the net effect <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;theta_g=&#92;sum_{j&#92;in g}a_jw_j &#92;)</span>.

**Proposition 1.** Let <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( w&#92;in&#92;mathbb{R}^p &#92;)</span>, let <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( u &#92;)</span> be the dual feasible point built from its residual in Section [3.1](#sec-formulation), and let <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G=P(w)-D(u) &#92;)</span>. The equicorrelation set <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( E=&#92;{j:&#124;x_j^&#92;top r^&#92;star&#124;=n&#92;lambda&#92;} &#92;)</span> is contained in <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;bar E=&#92;{j:&#124;x_j^&#92;top u&#124;+&#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;_2&#92;sqrt{2nG}&#92;ge n&#92;lambda&#92;} &#92;)</span>.
{: #prop-enclose}

**Proof.** The dual optimum is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( u^&#92;star=r^&#92;star &#92;)</span>. <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( D &#92;)</span> is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( (1/n) &#92;)</span>-strongly concave and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( u &#92;)</span> is feasible, so <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( D(u^&#92;star)-D(u)&#92;ge&#92;&#124;u-u^&#92;star&#92;&#124;_2^2/(2n) &#92;)</span>. Since <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( D(u^&#92;star)=P^&#92;star&#92;le P(w) &#92;)</span>, this gives <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;&#124;u-u^&#92;star&#92;&#124;_2^2&#92;le2nG &#92;)</span>. For <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( j&#92;in E &#92;)</span>, <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( n&#92;lambda=&#124;x_j^&#92;top u^&#92;star&#124;&#92;le&#124;x_j^&#92;top u&#124;+&#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;_2&#92;&#124;u-u^&#92;star&#92;&#124;_2&#92;le&#124;x_j^&#92;top u&#124;+&#92;&#124;x_j&#92;&#124;_2&#92;sqrt{2nG} &#92;)</span>. <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;square &#92;)</span>

## 4 Experimental Setup
{: #sec-setup}

**Synthetic experiments.** Each design has <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( n=500 &#92;)</span> rows and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( p=1000 &#92;)</span> features split into equicorrelated blocks of size 20 and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho&#92;in&#92;{0,0.3,0.6,0.9&#92;} &#92;)</span>. The number of true nonzeros is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( s&#92;in&#92;{5,20,40&#92;} &#92;)</span>, laid out either dispersed with one signal per active block or clustered with five signals per active block. All signals have amplitude 1 with paired mixed signs, and the noise level is <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;sigma=1 &#92;)</span>. We set the penalty to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;lambda=c&#92;sigma&#92;sqrt{2&#92;log p/n} &#92;)</span> and try <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( c&#92;in&#92;{0.5,1,2&#92;} &#92;)</span>. Over 5 paired seeds, the grid contains 360 targets. Each of the 4 methods solves all of them to <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}}&#92;le10^{-6} &#92;)</span>, for a total of 1440 solves.

A second experiment checks the choice of tolerance. While <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( s=20 &#92;)</span>, and <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X &#92;)</span> has either block correlation with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho&#92;in&#92;{0,0.9&#92;} &#92;)</span> or Toeplitz correlation with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho=0.9 &#92;)</span>. Two layouts, two signal strengths, and 20 new seeds bring the count to 240 targets. For each target, FISTA and CD converge to a gap of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-10} &#92;)</span> and store the coefficients at preset checkpoints.

**Real data tasks.** UJIIndoorLoc [[8]](#ref-8) predicts longitude from WiFi signal strengths. Each of 3 random splits trains on 11,962 rows and keeps about 452 columns. Each split uses the <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;lambda &#92;)</span> selected on its validation set. Leukemia [[31]](#ref-31) is a <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 72&#92;times7129 &#92;)</span> gene expression matrix, solved at <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;lambda/&#92;lambda_{&#92;max}=0.01 &#92;)</span> with three timing repeats. On both tasks every method runs without a time limit until <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}}&#92;le10^{-6} &#92;)</span>.

**UJI semi-synthetic tasks.** To obtain a known true support on a real correlation structure, we draw 1000 rows from a fixed UJI training pool, remove constant columns, keep the original WAP order, and generate 15 nonzero coefficients. Two layouts <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;times &#92;)</span> two signal strengths <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;times &#92;)</span> 10 seeds give 40 targets, with signals either all strong or mixed weak. For each target, sklearn and skglm each produce a reference solution with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}}&#92;le10^{-10} &#92;)</span>.

**External controls.** On Online News [[32]](#ref-32), with 1000 sampled rows and 58 features, and on Leukemia with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X &#92;)</span> fixed, we generate 5 nonzero coefficients with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( c&#92;in&#92;{0.5,1&#92;} &#92;)</span>, two signal strengths, and 9 seeds, giving 36 targets per source.

**Column order experiment.** We take 56 existing targets: 40 UJI, 8 news, and 8 gene. For each <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X &#92;)</span> we use the original order and 5 fixed random permutations <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;pi &#92;)</span>, solve on <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X[:,&#92;pi] &#92;)</span>, map the result back by <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( w_{&#92;pi(j)}=&#92;tilde w_j &#92;)</span>, and recompute <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}} &#92;)</span> in the original coordinates. Between runs, we change the column order and nothing else. The response, the penalty, the true coefficients, and the standardization all stay as they were, and every run has to reach <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( G_{&#92;mathrm{norm}}&#92;le10^{-10} &#92;)</span>. We collect 672 outputs this way and repeat the original order in 112 further runs to see whether a solver reproduces its own result. Swapping columns in problem [(1)](#eq-lasso) only renames the variables, so the set of optimal solutions stays the same. If the support differs between two orders, the solver has landed on a different point in a degenerate or nearly degenerate solution set.

### 5.4 Nearly Proportional Feature Groups and Order Sensitivity
{: #sec-5-4}

The UJI features are signal strengths of individual WiFi access points. After sampling 1000 rows, many access points are detected together in only a few rows, and their columns become nearly proportional, <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( x_j&#92;approx a_jx_{&#92;mathrm{anchor}} &#92;)</span>. Within such a group <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( g &#92;)</span>, the fitted values and the <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;ell_1 &#92;)</span> penalty stay nearly unchanged as long as the net group effect <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;theta_g=&#92;sum_{j&#92;in g}a_jw_j &#92;)</span> is fixed. The solution set is then nearly degenerate along directions inside a group, and the duality gap constrains these directions only weakly. Changing the column order changes the order in which coordinates are visited and how working sets are chosen, so the solver stops at a different point of the degenerate solution set. The location of the changed coordinates supports this explanation: all 27 changed coordinates under sklearn, and all 38 under skglm, lie in nearly proportional groups found by step 1 of the proposed method before fitting. After aggregating by net group effect, the two reference solutions agree on all 40 targets, with a maximum aggregated difference of about <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 4.5&#92;times10^{-10} &#92;)</span>. The 10 UJI targets on which the two references agreed exactly stay unchanged under every order.

The uniqueness condition for Lasso solutions explains why the news and gene data show no change under either implementation or threshold. Tibshirani [[3]](#ref-3) proved that the Lasso solution is unique when <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_E &#92;)</span>, with <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( E &#92;)</span> the equicorrelation set of Proposition [1](#prop-enclose), has full column rank, which holds automatically when the columns of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X &#92;)</span> are in general position. Two proportional columns have the same absolute correlation with any residual, so they enter <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( E &#92;)</span> together and make <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_E &#92;)</span> rank-deficient. The solution set then extends into a segment in the direction within the group, and the column order determines where on the segment the solver stops. UJI signal strengths are discrete, and undetected access points are recorded as a fixed value, so an access point detected in only one or two sampled rows becomes a duplicate column after standardization and falls into this case.

For the news and gene data, we apply step 2 of the proposed method to the two highly accurate solutions of each of the 36 confirmation targets per source, 144 outputs in total. Table [5](#table-unique) gives the results. Both full matrices are rank-deficient: the 58 news features contain linear dependencies, such as weekday dummies that always sum to 1, which gives a numerical rank of 56, and the gene matrix has <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( p&#92;gg n &#92;)</span> and a numerical rank of 71. All 144 submatrices <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( X_{&#92;bar E} &#92;)</span> have full column rank, with smallest singular values of at least 1.18, a clear margin from rank deficiency. The certificate therefore holds for every output, the solutions are unique, the solver converges to the same point from every column order, and the corresponding counts in Table [4](#table-perm) are 0.

## 6 Discussion and Limitations
{: #sec-discussion}

When a design contains nearly proportional feature groups, the objective leaves the split of coefficients within each group free, and the solver path decides it. Recovery scores computed from a single solve then include a path component, which moved F1 by as much as 9.63 percentage points on our UJI tasks. The proposed procedure detects this case at low cost. The group scan takes at most 0.16 s per design in our experiments, and the certificate needs one solution and one SVD, against six full solves per target in the column order experiment. Reporting F1 on the full task set together with its range across column orders captures both the recovery level and the size of the path component.

The order effect appeared on UJI, where discrete signal strengths and rarely detected access points produce duplicate columns. Designs with many rarely nonzero discrete features are the next cases to test, together with experiments that separate the effects of coordinate order, working set selection, and rounding. The elastic net makes the objective strongly convex, and the group Lasso selects variables by group, so both remove this degeneracy at the model level and are natural candidates for comparison.

## 7 Conclusion
{: #sec-conclusion}

Under a common duality gap stopping rule, stronger feature correlation raised the Lipschitz constant and worsened the conditioning near the optimum. At <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho=0.9 &#92;)</span>, ISTA, FISTA, and cyclic coordinate descent each took at least ten times as long as at <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( &#92;rho=0 &#92;)</span>, and skglm took 1.4 to 3.2 times as long. ISTA and CD converged linearly near the solution, at rates set by the curvature on the selected support. Supports on the synthetic designs settled by a gap of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-6} &#92;)</span>. On UJIIndoorLoc, reordering the input columns still changed the selected variables at a gap of <span class="tape-inline-math">&#92;( 10^{-10} &#92;)</span>. Every change occurred within a nearly proportional feature group, and the outputs agreed once coefficients were summed by group. The proposed procedure checks uniqueness from one approximate solution. Its duality gap bound certified a unique solution for all news and gene outputs, and on UJI the detected groups moved the evaluation to the group level.
